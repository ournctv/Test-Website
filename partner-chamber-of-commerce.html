About the Nantucket Island Chamber of Commerce:

The Nantucket Chamber is the local Chamber of Commerce as well as the Regional Tourism Council for the Commonwealth of Massachusetts serving Nantucket Island. We are the central point of contact for national, local, and international press.

Nantucket Chamber members reap the benefits of a wide variety of programs and services specifically tailored to serve our unique community. As a local, regional, and international destination marketing hub, we attract visitors from across the globe and drive business for all of our members. In addition, the Nantucket Chamber provides legislative liaison, professional development programs, networking events, business consultations, year round visitor information and referrals, and top quality collateral, such as publications, a destination website, social media marketing, and more.

2024 Tourism Advocate of the Year – Nantucket W.A.V.E.

The town manager, Libby Gibson, presents the Nantucket Regional Transit Authority. She gives its history: created under state law in 1993, in service from 1995 with four buses on two seasonal routes, and running year-round since April 27, 2018. It now runs three year-round and six seasonal routes, reaching Madaket, Miacomet, the airport and Sconset. She reports that joining the state’s Try Transit pilot made peak-season rides free and lifted ridership by nearly eighty per cent, that the diesel fleet is being replaced with electric buses, and that the door-to-door Your Island Ride service for seniors and people with disabilities has run year-round since 2001. The W.A.V.E. carried 267,000 passengers from June through August.

The 2019 Nantucket Experience

A day of training for island employees, in two parts. In the first, Jason Bridges – a Select Board member who runs a bike tour company and a coffee shop – teaches customer service. He covers four habits that raise it, beginning with smiling at whoever walks in; mindset; difficult customers, with a role-play; and what a gossip culture does to a workplace. The second part is a run of speakers on the island itself, so that staff can answer visitors’ questions. A historian traces the Wampanoag presence and the fisheries that drew it, the English settlers’ failed plan for sheep, the rise of whaling and the five factors behind its decline, and the tourism and artist colonies that followed. An arts speaker counts more than eleven galleries and runs through the island’s festival calendar. David Gagnon of the Maria Mitchell Association covers wind, wave height and fog, and how to read them against the ferry schedule. A last speaker covers practical knowledge, down to where the public restrooms are.

Hiring 02554: The Ins and Outs of Hiring on Nantucket

A panel on hiring, the first of a new Chamber series. Four employers take part: a Select Board member who runs a coffee shop, the general manager of a hotel that staffs forty to fifty off season and 125 in summer, a restaurant manager, and a town administrator with about five hundred employees. Asked the hardest part of hiring here, they set housing aside as a subject of its own and name the size of the labor pool, retention, and a season that shuts the island down. The coffee shop owner describes writing his interview questions out of his own stated core values, because one wrong hire in twelve can spoil a summer. The hotel hires for warmth and trains the skills afterwards, cross-trains staff between departments, and warns that deducting housing can push a paycheck below minimum wage. Other threads: reading resumes, finding what a candidate is passionate about, onboarding, when to let a bad hire go mid-season, recruiting from abroad, and building an employer brand on social media.

Chamber Workshop: Tools to Be Successful While Working Remote

A workshop in two halves. The first runs through software for working remotely: project management tools, meeting and messaging tools, file sharing, password managers, workflow automation and online bookkeeping. The presenter explains boards, calendars and task assignment by example, and takes questions throughout. The second half covers marketing to customers in the off season or while closed: claiming and updating a Google Business listing, choosing the few platforms where your customers already are, and keeping a website that works on a phone. How Women Can Accomplish More by Doing Less

A nurse practitioner from Lavender Farm Wellness presents on three things she treats as a baseline: hydration, fasting and sleep. She says at the start that the talk is educational and not a substitute for medical advice. On water she explains how the body signals the kidneys to hold on to it, how to read urine color, and cites a study in which mild dehydration made simple tasks feel harder, and another in which raising intake improved migraine scores. Her practical version is two glasses in the morning and two at night, with more when menstruating, breastfeeding, pregnant, or drinking coffee or alcohol. On fasting she separates intermittent fasting from time-restricted eating, ties the benefit to circadian rhythm, and cautions that fasting can trigger a history of disordered eating. On sleep she covers blue light, caffeine, and alcohol as a stimulant that suppresses melatonin. Questions follow on cold plunging, elimination diets and gut bacteria.

Daffodil Festival

One Million Daffodils

A history of the Daffodil Festival, built around footage of the antique car parade. The narration traces it to the fall of 1974, when Jean MacAusland put the idea to the Nantucket Garden Club, and to the first show the following April – 260 daffodils at the Boys Club on a rainy Friday. The goal she set, a million daffodils planted across the island, took six years; the narration puts the count now above three million. The parade’s growth is traced too: nineteen cars in the first Garden Club Vintage Motor Car Outing, more than thirty-five by 1981, and eighty registered two years later with another forty turning up anyway. The owner of a 1927 American LaFrance fire engine, in the parade since the start, describes how much shorter the early route was. Participants are interviewed along the way, several of them on the festival returning after two years away. The parade ends in Sconset for the tailgate picnic.

International Women’s Day

Women in Business – March 8, 2019

The Chamber’s first International Women’s Day event, held with Petticoat Row and Nantucket Women in Business. Denice Kronau, formerly chief diversity officer at Siemens, moderates a panel of five island business leaders: the executive director of Remain Nantucket, the executive director of a wellness network, the owner of a salon who is also a partner in Lemon Press, the general manager of Cisco Brewers, and the owner of the ’Sconset General Store, who taught yoga for twenty years before that. The year’s theme was “Balance for Better,” and the first question from the room challenges the word balance itself as something women use against themselves. The panel takes questions on prioritizing, on mentors found outside one’s own field, on definitions of success that change with the decade, on gender roles at home, and on the cost of being the only woman in a role. Business books come up: Danny Meyer, Seth Godin, Malcolm Gladwell, Jim Collins. It closes with a Ruth Bader Ginsburg line on how many women on the Supreme Court would be enough, and with the three women in the room running for Select Board.

International Women’s Day Breakfast 2020

The Chamber’s second annual International Women’s Day breakfast, held with the Nantucket Historical Association. The NHA’s director previews its programming for the centenary of the 19th Amendment, including a newly bought sculpture of Lucretia Mott and a proposal to fill the Hadwen House garden with sculptures of notable island women. Denice Kronau moderates a panel of three. Emily Molden describes becoming an environmental advocate after years of solitary field work – an endangered alpine butterfly in Colorado, bird research on Muskeget – in spite of a fear of public speaking. Wendy Hudson describes arriving in 1988, working at Bartlett’s Farm during college, and going on to own Nantucket Bookworks and Mitchell’s Book Corner. The third panelist describes coming from Haiti by way of Costa Rica, years spent in a convent, and a move from banking into mental health counseling. The questions cover leadership styles, advice to a twenty-one-year-old self, and failure. One panelist describes a manager telling her outright that he did not want to hire more people of color.

International Women’s Day 2021 with Denella J. Clark

The Chamber’s third annual International Women’s Day celebration. Denella J. Clark, president of the Boston Arts Academy Foundation and the first Black woman to chair the Massachusetts Commission on the Status of Women, presents the Commission’s work through the pandemic: an action team formed in March 2020, a virtual hearing and survey, a hearing on child care, and an event on the pandemic’s effect on women of color with Representative Ayanna Pressley. She reports the findings. Of the women surveyed, 53 per cent had cut their hours or were considering it, 39 per cent said child care and school changes had affected their work, and 21 per cent had considered quitting. She covers domestic violence that was rising and going unreported, food and housing insecurity, delayed health care, and concern for children’s social and educational progress. She notes that the 140,000 jobs the country lost that December were all held by women. Questions follow on remote participation in public meetings and on universal pre-kindergarten in Massachusetts.

International Women’s Day Breakfast 2023

The Chamber’s International Women’s Day breakfast, held with the Nantucket Historical Association. The moderator, executive director of the Nantucket Book Foundation, opens with a history of the day: recognised by the United Nations in 1977, but rooted in the labor movements before it, beginning with the National Women’s Day observed in the United States in February 1909 to mark the garment workers’ strike in New York the year before. She names island women who campaigned for suffrage and against slavery, Lucretia Mott and Maria Mitchell among them. The year’s United Nations theme was innovation and technology for gender equality, and the panel takes it four ways. Dr. Elizabeth Hallett, the superintendent of schools, describes choosing classroom software that ties to standards rather than to novelty, and teaching students to be good digital citizens. The NHA’s director of collections describes digitising the collection, and the cost of then caring for an object twice over, in storage and as files. A bank’s financial crimes officer describes fraud prevention, and tells the room the bank will never ask for an online banking password. A publisher describes the move from print to digital. Questions follow on the gender gap in coding and in gaming.

International Women’s Day Breakfast 2026

The Chamber’s International Women’s Day breakfast, on the theme “Give to Gain.” Denice Kronau moderates, opening with Sir Isaac Newton on standing on the shoulders of giants and framing the morning as a celebration as much as a call to action. Four panelists introduce themselves: Megan Calabrese, who founded a real estate management company with a business partner and acquired an island property manager inside its first year; Sarah Bois, director of research and conservation at the Linda Loring Nature Foundation, who began her conservation career as an intern at the Maria Mitchell Association in the late 1990s; Liz Cordero, a bookkeeper of seventeen years and a business owner for seven; and Stephanie Serra, NCTV’s director of programming, who leads the room through a short grounding exercise and describes empowerment as a process running from the individual outward through small circles into the community. Cordero names her mother, who arrived in her mid-twenties with two small children and a sixth-grade education and bought her own house. Mentorship runs through the rest: being the only woman at the table early in a career, a mentor who asked only whether the people she worked for were good people, listening for what someone actually needs, and interns who go on to mentor the high school volunteers. There is an icebreaker round and a speed round. Kronau closes on Margaret Mead.

Pitch Competition

The Second Annual Chamber Pitch Competition

Four island teams pitch to three judges at the Dreamland in a format modeled on Shark Tank and run by N.I.C.E., the Chamber’s entrepreneurship center. The judges are Bruce Percelay, publisher of N Magazine; Elin Hilderbrand; and Jamie Siminoff, inventor of the Ring video doorbell. John Shea hosts. The pitches: Nantucket Crisps, a potato chip brand whose flavors are named for island beaches and whose bags would carry a code linking to a map of them; Nantucket Captains Camp, a paid summer program at Madaket Marine teaching navigation, engine work, aquaculture and bookkeeping to young women, argued from the claim that two percent of the world’s seafarers are women and from the marina’s own difficulty staffing its docks; Nantucket Faraway Chocolate, a bean-to-bar factory, cafe and chocolate bar sourcing cacao from small growers; and an online gallery for island artists, working title Faraway Island Gallery, which would frame and ship the work and then scale to comparable markets. The judges press on making the chips off island, on admissions and diversity, on price points, and on the cost of online advertising. A check-in covers the first competition’s winner, the bike-share Sandy Pedals. Nantucket Crisps takes the ten thousand dollar first prize, and the other three teams split the remaining prizes.

The Third Annual Chamber Pitch Competition

Four island entrepreneurs pitch to three judges for a share of twenty thousand dollars, in a format modeled on Shark Tank and run by N.I.C.E., the Chamber’s entrepreneurship center. The judges are Jamie Siminoff, inventor of the Ring video doorbell; Elin Hilderbrand; and Bruce Percelay, publisher of N Magazine. John Shea hosts. The pitches: Nantucket Ocean Brew, a ready-to-drink cocktail built on jun kombucha; Salvage Nantucket, which would catalog and grade historic material pulled out of island renovations and resell it rather than send it to the dump; Broke Da Mouth Salsa, made with produce and seconds from island farms and looking for a co-packer to scale; and Pamper, a platform pairing visitors with local couriers for on-demand errands. Each pitch draws questions from the judges on pricing, competitors and margins. Update videos cover two earlier winners, one of them a camp that has become a young women’s marine science academy. Broke Da Mouth Salsa takes the ten thousand dollar grand prize, and the other three teams split the remaining prizes.

Talk of the Town

Talk of the Town: Wendy Hudson

A short profile recorded at Mitchell’s Book Corner during the week of the 15th Nantucket Book Festival. Wendy Hudson gives her own history in island business. She has sold books on Nantucket since 1995, bought Nantucket Book Works in 2000 and Mitchell’s in 2012, and founded Cisco Brewers with her husband. She is a returning Chamber board member and a past president. She makes the case that every island business should join: the Chamber advertises the island off-island, and the Daffodil Festival and Christmas Stroll are purely its work, which she credits with carrying the shoulder seasons. She is also vice chair of the Retailers Association of Massachusetts, which she joined over sales tax equity when Amazon was not required to collect it. She names employee housing and the rising cost of health insurance as the hardest problems facing small businesses here. Her answer to both is that independent businesses gain leverage by banding together in trade associations. Asked what makes something last, she says it is how you treat your employees and your customers.
